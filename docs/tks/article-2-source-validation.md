# A Brain Map Can Look Right. How Would We Know It Is?

*Six studies, six different kinds of evidence, and the question behind every bright spot on a brain image*

A brain map can be persuasive before anyone explains how it was checked. A coloured region appears in the expected place. The estimated signal follows the task. Another analysis gives a similar picture. It is tempting to read the map as a photograph of the active tissue.

But a source map is an inference from measurements taken elsewhere. An algorithm asks which internal activity could have produced the recorded signal, under a model of the head and assumptions about the activity itself. Different internal explanations can fit. The practical question is therefore not whether the picture looks anatomically reasonable. It is **what independent observation would have made the picture fail if it were wrong**.

I read six primary studies that approach that question from different angles. Some know where an electrical pulse was delivered. One records neural events simultaneously inside the brain and at the scalp. One compares maps with surgery and patient outcome. Another studies what reconstruction algorithms do to known, constructed activity patterns. The sixth changes the assumed electrical properties of the head while holding the measured response in view. Together, they show why no single form of validation is enough and how a stronger test could be designed.

This is a follow-up to my earlier [Learn article](https://medium.com/@tranav50/can-we-reconstruct-everything-the-brain-is-doing-without-going-inside-3c7577f52ba8), which introduces EEG, brain signals and the forward and inverse problems. Here I want to stay with the harder next step: **what would make a particular anatomical claim credible?**

## Start with a location we actually know

The cleanest way to test a location estimate seems obvious: put a signal at a known location and ask the algorithm where it came from. That is what intracranial stimulation offers. During clinical monitoring, electrodes are already implanted for medical reasons. A brief current pulse can be delivered between two adjacent contacts. Their position can be found in a scan, and scalp EEG records the resulting electrical field.

[Mikulan and colleagues' Localize-MI study](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41597-020-0467-x.pdf) assembled such recordings from **seven people and 61 stimulation sessions**, together with MRI, implanted-contact positions and high-density scalp EEG. The paper's Fig. 2 maps the stimulation sites and imaging data. In Fig. 3, the authors try source-imaging methods and settings, including different channel counts, and compare estimated locations with the contact positions. For each session's most favourable parameter configuration, the average minimum distance to the contact site was **5.39 mm**. Across parameter configurations, some distances extended to roughly **50 mm**. Both observations matter. The first shows why known sites make a method testable; the second shows why reporting only the best setting can give a misleading impression of routine accuracy.

There is also a boundary that the authors state plainly. The pulse produces an **electrical stimulation artifact**, not an ordinary spontaneous neural event. We know where current was injected, but the resulting field need not have the spatial extent, orientation or timing of a population of neurons acting together. The experiment can test a forward model, electrode registration and an inverse pipeline on an imposed-input problem. It cannot, by itself, prove that the same pipeline recovers endogenous neural sources with the same accuracy.

[Unnwongse and colleagues](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9942548/) used the known-contact idea to probe a second weakness: the forward model's assumed tissue conductivity. They enrolled **11 patients**, obtained analyzable stimulation averages from **nine**, and recorded with **37 scalp electrodes**. Their Fig. 1 walks through CT-to-MRI contact registration and the construction of individual finite-element head models. They fitted dipoles to averaged pulse potentials while varying skull conductivity across **11 settings**. The paper reports **3,619 accepted fits**, but these came from repeated model settings applied to **341 contact-pair averages**. Treating 3,619 as independent people, or even independent stimulation sites, would greatly exaggerate the amount of independent evidence.

Depending on source depth and skull conductivity, mean localization errors ranged from **10.3 to 26 mm**. Deeper sources and lower assumed skull conductivity generally increased error. The known contact location made that sensitivity measurable. Yet even a correct contact location did not make the head's electrical properties known. The authors explicitly leave open whether their conductivity findings transfer to true neural signals. The lesson is that *ground truth for one part of an experiment does not settle every modeling assumption in it*.

## Now replace the pulse with a neural event

To move closer to the question most people care about, researchers need a measured neural event with some independent spatial reference. [Zauli and colleagues](https://air.unimi.it/retrieve/e5cb014e-6527-4977-8590-6d80f9f288e3/1-s2.0-S0165027024001389-main.pdf) recorded high-density scalp EEG and implanted stereo-EEG simultaneously in **eight people** being evaluated for epilepsy. Four had hippocampal discharges and four had focal cortical dysplasia activity. The implanted recordings identified the timing of interictal epileptiform discharges, or IEDs, which were often difficult to recognize in single scalp trials.

The study used those internal event times to align and average the scalp recordings. Fig. 2 is revealing: individual scalp traces look unconvincing in many cases, while the aligned average develops a visible pattern. The average was built from **82.5 IEDs per person** on average, although the count varied substantially between people. Only **9.4%** of intracranially identified events were detectable from scalp recordings alone on average. Thus, a successful map after SEEG-guided averaging does not imply that the method can locate each event from an unaided single scalp recording.

The authors then compared dSPM and eLORETA estimates with the implanted contact where the IED was identified. Across methods and settings, the mean distance was **35.56 mm**. Taking the best result for each participant lowered the mean to **19.20 mm**. Fig. 3 and Table 2 show the variation across people and choices. This is far more directly relevant to neural activity than a stimulation pulse, but the reference is still incomplete. An SEEG contact records activity near its sampled location; it does not survey every potentially active region. A discharge seen at one contact may be part of a wider or propagating event. The best-setting number also cannot be assumed to be the result of a method fixed before seeing the answer.

I find this study particularly valuable because it changes the question from “Can the algorithm find a known injected current?” to “Can scalp information point towards a neural event observed independently?” The answer is encouraging in this small cohort, especially after averaging, but its precise scope matters. It is evidence about **SEEG-timed averages of selected epileptic events**, under the tested models and parameter choices. It is not a demonstration that arbitrary brain activity can be mapped with two-centimetre accuracy.

## A patient outcome asks a different question

In epilepsy care, a map matters if it helps identify tissue relevant to seizures and treatment. [Vorderwülbecke and colleagues' 2020 study](https://cibm.ch/wp-content/uploads/1-s2.0-S1388245720304934-main.pdf) brings a different endpoint into the picture. It examined **45 surgical patients** with presurgical EEG acquired from **257 channels**, individual MRI, resection information and **12-month outcomes**. Thirty patients had favourable outcomes. The authors compared source estimates from averaged interictal discharges across several channel selections and event time points. Their usual analysis retained **204 channels**, excluding the cheek and neck electrodes most prone to artifacts.

Among favourable-outcome patients, the source maximum with the 204-channel setup was within **10 mm** of the resection in **67%**, and within an affected sublobe in **83%**. Fig. 1 defines the channel layouts and analysis choices; Figs. 2–4 and Table 1 show how conclusions vary with montage, anatomical group and the point on the discharge's rising phase used for localization. The half-rise point performed best in this comparison. This endpoint has an advantage that an imposed pulse lacks: whether a person did well after surgery matters clinically.

But it answers a different question from “Was this the exact neural generator?” Surgeons may remove a region larger than the tissue that generated an interictal discharge. Several connected regions may contribute to both the scalp event and the seizures. A favourable outcome after a resection supports a useful anatomical hypothesis without identifying every active millimetre. Conversely, the authors caution that a poor outcome does not establish that the source estimate was wrong: surgery can fail for other reasons. The study has no simultaneous intracranial reference for these events. It is a valuable clinical comparison precisely because its endpoint and its limitations are distinct.

These first four papers therefore form a ladder rather than a ranking from “bad” to “good.” Contact stimulation gives a sharp input location but an artificial signal. Simultaneous SEEG gives a real neural event but samples only implanted regions. Surgical outcome asks whether a source hypothesis connects to treatment, but the resection is a coarse spatial reference. Each can falsify a different claim. None should be made to answer a question its endpoint cannot resolve.

Their error figures also should not be placed in a league table. A best-case distance from a stimulation contact, the average distance from an SEEG channel after event-guided averaging, and the distance to a resection boundary are measured against different targets. The participants, electrode layouts, source models and tuning procedures differ. Even the meaning of “one case” changes: Unnwongse's thousands of fits arise from hundreds of contact-pair averages under multiple conductivity settings, while the surgical study counts people and outcomes. A smaller number in one column would not prove that method is better. I would compare methods within a study's common input and endpoint first, then ask whether the conclusion survives a different endpoint.

## Check what the algorithm would do even if the input were known

There is another way to challenge a map before seeking a measured neural ground truth: give an inverse algorithm a source pattern that *we* construct, pass it through a forward model and see how the algorithm changes it. [Hauk and colleagues](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7793168/) built an empirical resolution analysis using MRI and resting EEG/MEG recordings from **five healthy participants**. They divided each cortical hemisphere into **500 patches**, activated them one at a time in the model, mixed their predicted measurements with recorded resting activity and compared several inverse methods.

Their Fig. 2 explains the empirical resolution matrix: for a proposed active patch, where does the method put the estimated activity? Fig. 4 displays patterns of spread and cross-talk; Figs. 5–7 look at peak location, spatial dispersion and active-versus-inactive classification as signal-to-noise ratio changes. The results are not a single number for “EEG resolution.” Error depends on location, source pattern, noise level and estimator. A method can put a peak near the right location while spreading activity widely. Another can look spatially sharp yet miss the active patch or leak into a distant region.

This type of test has an enormous strength: the constructed source is known exactly, and many patches can be evaluated systematically. Its weakness is equally important: the brain did not produce those constructed patches. Their shapes and amplitudes are assumptions. Recorded resting data make the noise environment more realistic, but they do not turn the injected model signal into an observed endogenous source. Resolution analysis tells us what an algorithm would do **if** its specified source and forward model were right. It is essential for interpreting a map, yet it cannot alone verify the map's biological cause.

My own [Observable Geometry Build](https://github.com/tranav4464/observable-geometry) asks an even earlier, narrower model question: whether distant single-source candidates have similar predicted scalp patterns before an inverse method is chosen. It does not replace Hauk's estimator-specific resolution measures or the measured-event studies above. Reading them together made a useful distinction clearer to me: a forward-model ambiguity and an inverse algorithm's leakage are related, but neither is a measurement of where a particular person's neurons were active.

## What if the head model is wrong?

All of these comparisons depend on how the head is represented. [Vorwerk and colleagues](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/neuroscience/articles/10.3389/fnins.2019.00531/full) studied a measured somatosensory evoked response while varying uncertain electrical conductivities for skin, skull, cerebrospinal fluid, gray matter and white matter. Their individual head model used MRI and digitized electrodes. Instead of recalculating a costly forward model from scratch for every possible conductivity setting, they used a checked generalized polynomial-chaos approximation to evaluate many settings.

Fig. 1 shows the measured response. Table 1 declares the conductivity ranges. Figs. 4 and 7–10 show how fitted source positions and other fit properties change across those ranges. In this case, skin and skull uncertainty had especially strong effects on reconstructed location and depth. The EEG response had not changed; the **assumed path from source to electrode** had. The authors caution that the best-fitting conductivities in this particular single-dipole scenario are not universal values, and that the chosen uncertainty intervals and other modeling errors matter.

This is the challenge that can quietly undermine a reassuring validation plot. If one head model makes a source appear in region A and another plausible model makes it appear in region B, a map produced under the first is not uniquely supported by the scalp data alone. A strong validation should ask whether its conclusion survives a justified range of electrode registrations, tissue conductivities, source orientations and noise estimates. “Justified” is important: an arbitrarily wide uncertainty range can make every claim fail, while an artificially narrow range hides genuine uncertainty. That range needs independent measurements or a defensible protocol.

## What would convince me?

I would first ask the researcher to state the *claim* at the right scale. “The estimated peak falls in this exact cortical vertex,” “this region is more plausible than that region,” and “the map is useful for planning treatment” are different claims. They need different references. A known stimulation contact can test an imposed-input localization method. Simultaneous intracranial recordings can support a claim about measured neural events near sampled contacts. Surgical outcome can test clinical usefulness. None is interchangeable with the others.

Next, I would want the model and analysis fixed before the decisive result is inspected: channel selection, reference, MRI-to-electrode registration, conductivity treatment, source family, inverse settings and the metric used to judge success. If the best algorithm and parameters are selected separately for each test case, that is a useful *development* result, but it is an optimistic estimate of future performance. A later participant held out from those choices is a stronger test.

The comparison should include credible alternatives. A new method needs to beat or complement a matched established approach, not merely produce a convincing image. Report not only peak distance but also spatial spread, missed activity, wrong confident attributions and uncertainty when competing locations remain plausible. Those quantities answer different questions, as Hauk's resolution analysis illustrates. If the same measured data can support two distant sources under plausible models, the honest output may be **ambiguous**, not a forced bright spot.

I would also separate **agreement between methods** from **agreement with reality**. Two inverse methods may share the same leadfield, MRI registration, covariance and source assumptions. Their maps can agree because they inherit the same error. Conversely, methods may disagree for understandable reasons even when both are internally consistent. An independent endpoint, and a test that varies the shared assumptions, tells us more than a vote among similar-looking maps. This is why the conductivity study belongs beside the clinical and intracranial studies rather than in a technical appendix.

Finally, the reference itself should be audited. Were scalp electrodes correctly aligned to that person's MRI? Does the implanted contact sample the alleged origin or only a nearby part of a wider network? Does the time window capture the event's beginning or a propagated response? Is a surgical cavity being treated as a point? These details are not footnotes. They determine what “correct location” means.

The experiment I would most like to see combines the strengths of these studies without pretending any one reference is complete. It would use **simultaneous measured scalp and intracranial neural events** across several participants, independently checked individual geometry, a model-uncertainty range fixed in advance, and a source-reporting rule that can explicitly decline to name a unique region. The development participants would set parameters; held-out participants would test them. Direct leadfield comparison, established resolution measures and conventional inverse methods would be matched baselines. The principal outcome would be whether the rule makes **fewer confidently wrong anatomical claims at a stated coverage level**, with intracranial sampling limits reported rather than ignored. A surgical endpoint could provide a separate test of clinical usefulness.

That experiment would still not reveal every active neuron. It would, however, put a sharper burden of proof on a map. It asks whether the location survives independent evidence, plausible changes in the model and a comparison with what researchers can already do. That is the standard I would want before treating a beautiful brain image as an explanation of what happened inside it.
