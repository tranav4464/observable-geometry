# A Brain Map Can Look Right. How Would We Know It Is?

*Six studies that test what a brain map can really tell us*

A brain map can be persuasive before anyone explains how it was checked. A coloured region appears in the expected place. The estimated signal follows the task. Another analysis gives a similar picture. It is tempting to read the map as a photograph of the active tissue.

But a source map is an inference from measurements taken elsewhere. An algorithm asks which internal activity could have produced the recorded signal, under a model of the head and assumptions about the activity itself. Different internal explanations can fit. If the map points to one region, **what observation could show that location is wrong?**

I chose six studies because each checks a different part of a brain map. Two begin with a known electrical stimulation site. One records neural events inside the brain and at the scalp at the same time. Another compares source maps with surgery and patient outcome. One tests reconstruction algorithms on deliberately constructed activity, and the last asks what happens when the head's assumed electrical properties change. I wanted to know what each test could actually rule out.

In my earlier [Learn article](https://medium.com/@tranav50/can-we-reconstruct-everything-the-brain-is-doing-without-going-inside-3c7577f52ba8), I explained how brain activity can become a scalp recording and why working backwards is difficult. I kept returning to one question afterward: **what would make a particular anatomical claim credible?**

## Start with a location we actually know

The cleanest way to test a location estimate seems obvious: put a signal at a known location and ask the algorithm where it came from. That is what intracranial stimulation offers. During clinical monitoring, electrodes are already implanted for medical reasons. A brief current pulse can be delivered between two adjacent contacts. Their position can be found in a scan, and scalp EEG records the resulting electrical field.

[Mikulan and colleagues' Localize-MI study](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41597-020-0467-x.pdf) assembled such recordings from **seven people and 61 stimulation sessions**, together with MRI, implanted-contact positions and high-density scalp EEG. The paper's Fig. 2 maps the stimulation sites and imaging data. In Fig. 3, the authors try source-imaging methods and settings, including different channel counts, and compare estimated locations with the contact positions. For each session's most favourable parameter configuration, the average minimum distance to the contact site was **5.39 mm**. Across parameter configurations, some distances extended to roughly **50 mm**. I would not treat the best-setting figure as the accuracy a new patient could expect; the result changes substantially with the choices made during analysis.

The authors also point out that the pulse produces an **electrical stimulation artifact**, not an ordinary spontaneous neural event. We know where current was injected, but the resulting field need not have the spatial extent, orientation or timing of a population of neurons acting together. The experiment can test a forward model, electrode registration and an inverse pipeline on an imposed-input problem. It cannot, by itself, prove that the same pipeline recovers endogenous neural sources with the same accuracy.

[Unnwongse and colleagues](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9942548/) used the known-contact idea to probe a second weakness: the forward model's assumed tissue conductivity, or how easily electrical current passes through each tissue. They enrolled **11 patients**, obtained analyzable stimulation averages from **nine**, and recorded with **37 scalp electrodes**. Their Fig. 1 walks through CT-to-MRI contact registration and the construction of individual finite-element head models, which divide the head into small pieces for electrical calculations. They fitted dipoles, simplified point-like electrical sources, to averaged pulse potentials while varying skull conductivity across **11 settings**. The paper reports **3,619 accepted fits**, but these came from repeated model settings applied to **341 contact-pair averages**. Treating 3,619 as independent people, or even independent stimulation sites, would greatly exaggerate the amount of independent evidence.

Depending on source depth and skull conductivity, mean localization errors ranged from **10.3 to 26 mm**. Deeper sources and lower assumed skull conductivity generally increased error. The known contact location made that sensitivity measurable, but it did not tell the researchers the head's true electrical properties. The authors leave open whether their conductivity findings transfer to neural signals. Knowing the input location answered one question while exposing another.

## Now replace the pulse with a neural event

A stimulation pulse leaves us wondering whether the method will work on neural activity. [Zauli and colleagues](https://air.unimi.it/retrieve/e5cb014e-6527-4977-8590-6d80f9f288e3/1-s2.0-S0165027024001389-main.pdf) recorded high-density scalp EEG and stereo-EEG, recordings from electrodes implanted inside the brain, simultaneously in **eight people** being evaluated for epilepsy. Four had discharges in the hippocampus and four had activity linked to focal cortical dysplasia, an area where the cortex developed abnormally. The implanted recordings identified the timing of interictal epileptiform discharges, or IEDs: brief abnormal electrical events between seizures. These were often difficult to recognize in single scalp trials.

The study used those internal event times to align and average the scalp recordings. Averaging repeated events helps a shared pattern stand out from unrelated fluctuations. Fig. 2 is revealing: individual scalp traces look unconvincing in many cases, while the aligned average develops a visible pattern. The average was built from **82.5 IEDs per person** on average, although the count varied substantially between people. Only **9.4%** of intracranially identified events were detectable from scalp recordings alone on average. Thus, a successful map after SEEG-guided averaging does not imply that the method can locate each event from an unaided single scalp recording.

The authors then compared estimates from two established source-imaging methods, dSPM and eLORETA, with the implanted contact where the IED was identified. Across methods and settings, the mean distance was **35.56 mm**. Taking the best result for each participant lowered the mean to **19.20 mm**. Fig. 3 and Table 2 show the variation across people and choices. This is far more directly relevant to neural activity than a stimulation pulse, but the reference is still incomplete. An implanted contact records activity near its sampled location; it does not survey every potentially active region. A discharge seen at one contact may be part of a wider or propagating event. The best-setting number also cannot be assumed to be the result of a method fixed before seeing the answer.

This was the first paper in the set that gave me an independent recording of the neural event itself. The scalp estimate often came closer to the implanted contact after averaging, but the SEEG supplied the event times and sampled only part of the brain. The result concerns **SEEG-timed averages of selected epileptic events** under these models and parameter choices. It does not show that an unaided scalp recording can map arbitrary activity with two-centimetre accuracy.

## A patient outcome asks a different question

In epilepsy care, a map matters if it helps identify tissue relevant to seizures and treatment. [Vorderwülbecke and colleagues' 2020 study](https://cibm.ch/wp-content/uploads/1-s2.0-S1388245720304934-main.pdf) brings a different endpoint into the picture. It examined **45 surgical patients** with presurgical EEG acquired from **257 channels**, individual MRI, information about the tissue removed in surgery, called the resection, and **12-month outcomes**. Thirty patients had favourable outcomes. The authors compared source estimates from averaged interictal discharges across several channel selections and event time points. Their usual analysis retained **204 channels**, excluding the cheek and neck electrodes most prone to artifacts.

Among favourable-outcome patients, the source maximum, the strongest point on the estimated map, was within **10 mm** of the resection in **67%** with the 204-channel setup, and within an affected sublobe, a smaller division of a brain lobe, in **83%**. Fig. 1 defines the channel layouts and analysis choices; Figs. 2–4 and Table 1 show how conclusions vary with montage, anatomical group and the point on the discharge's rising phase used for localization. The half-rise point performed best in this comparison. This endpoint has an advantage that an imposed pulse lacks: whether a person did well after surgery matters clinically.

But a good surgical outcome does not tell us the exact neural generator. Surgeons may remove a region larger than the tissue that generated an interictal discharge. Several connected regions may contribute to both the scalp event and the seizures. A favourable outcome supports a useful anatomical hypothesis without identifying every active millimetre. The authors also caution that a poor outcome does not establish that the source estimate was wrong: surgery can fail for other reasons. The study has no simultaneous intracranial reference for these events.

The four studies test different claims. Contact stimulation gives a sharp input location but an artificial signal. Simultaneous SEEG gives a real neural event but samples only implanted regions. Surgical outcome asks whether a source hypothesis connects to treatment, while the resection remains a coarse spatial reference. I would choose among these tests based on the anatomical claim being made.

Their error figures also should not be placed in a league table. A best-case distance from a stimulation contact, the average distance from an SEEG channel after event-guided averaging, and the distance to a resection boundary are measured against different targets. The participants, electrode layouts, source models and tuning procedures differ. Even the meaning of “one case” changes: Unnwongse's thousands of fits arise from hundreds of contact-pair averages under multiple conductivity settings, while the surgical study counts people and outcomes. A smaller number in one column would not prove that method is better. I would compare methods within a study's common input and endpoint first, then ask whether the conclusion survives a different endpoint.

I kept asking what could make each test look successful while the anatomical claim remained wrong. A stimulation study might locate its injected current accurately even if it handles neural events poorly. An SEEG-guided average might locate a discharge that a scalp-only analysis would never detect. A source maximum might fall inside a successful resection even if the resection contains much more tissue than the discharge's origin. These are not reasons to dismiss the studies. They tell us exactly which claim each result earns, and what the next experiment must check.

## Check what the algorithm would do even if the input were known

There is another way to challenge a map before seeking a measured neural ground truth: give an inverse algorithm a source pattern that *we* construct, pass it through a forward model and see how the algorithm changes it. [Hauk and colleagues](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7793168/) built an empirical resolution analysis using MRI and resting EEG/MEG recordings from **five healthy participants**. They divided each cortical hemisphere into **500 patches**, activated them one at a time in the model, mixed their predicted measurements with recorded resting activity and compared several inverse methods.

Their Fig. 2 explains the empirical resolution matrix: for a proposed active patch, where does the method put the estimated activity? Fig. 4 displays patterns of spread and cross-talk, where activity from one place appears in another estimate. Figs. 5–7 look at peak location, spatial dispersion, or how far the estimate spreads, and active-versus-inactive classification as signal strength relative to noise changes. The results are not a single number for “EEG resolution.” Error depends on location, source pattern, noise level and estimator. A method can put a peak near the right location while spreading activity widely. Another can look spatially sharp yet miss the active patch or leak into a distant region.

With constructed sources, the researchers know exactly what they put in and can test hundreds of patches systematically. But the brain did not produce those patches; their shapes and amplitudes were chosen for the test. Recorded resting data provide a realistic noise background without turning the injected model signal into an observed endogenous source. Resolution analysis tells us what an algorithm would do **if** its specified source and forward model were right. It cannot alone verify the biological cause of a person's map.

My [Observable Geometry Build](https://github.com/tranav4464/observable-geometry) asks whether distant single-source candidates have similar predicted scalp patterns before an inverse method is chosen. Hauk's analysis then shows what a chosen inverse method would do to constructed activity. Both can warn us about a map, but neither measures where a particular person's neurons were active.

## What if the head model is wrong?

All of these comparisons depend on how the head is represented. [Vorwerk and colleagues](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/neuroscience/articles/10.3389/fnins.2019.00531/full) studied a measured somatosensory evoked response, the brain's electrical response to a touch stimulus, while varying uncertain electrical conductivities for skin, skull, cerebrospinal fluid, gray matter and white matter. Their individual head model used MRI and digitized electrodes. Instead of recalculating a costly forward model from scratch for every possible conductivity setting, they used a checked mathematical shortcut called a generalized polynomial-chaos approximation.

Fig. 1 shows the measured response. Table 1 declares the conductivity ranges. Figs. 4 and 7–10 show how fitted source positions and other fit properties change across those ranges. In this case, skin and skull uncertainty had especially strong effects on reconstructed location and depth. The EEG response had not changed; the **assumed path from source to electrode** had. The authors caution that the best-fitting conductivities in this particular single-dipole scenario are not universal values, and that the chosen uncertainty intervals and other modeling errors matter.

If one plausible head model places a source in region A and another places it in region B, the scalp data alone do not settle which location to report. I would want to know whether the conclusion survives justified changes in electrode registration, tissue conductivity, source orientation and noise estimates. The range matters: make it too wide and every claim fails; make it too narrow and the test misses genuine uncertainty. Independent measurements or a prespecified protocol should set those ranges.

## What would convince me?

I would first ask the researcher to state the *claim* at the right scale. “The estimated peak falls in this exact cortical vertex,” “this region is more plausible than that region,” and “the map is useful for planning treatment” are different claims. They need different references. A known stimulation contact can test an imposed-input localization method. Simultaneous intracranial recordings can support a claim about measured neural events near sampled contacts. Surgical outcome can test clinical usefulness. None is interchangeable with the others.

Next, I would want the model and analysis fixed before the decisive result is inspected: channel selection, reference, alignment of electrodes to the person's MRI, conductivity treatment, the source patterns the model allows, inverse settings and the measure used to judge success. If the best algorithm and parameters are selected separately for each test case, that is a useful *development* result, but it is an optimistic estimate of future performance. A later participant held out from those choices is a stronger test.

The comparison should include credible alternatives. A new method needs to beat or complement a matched established approach, not merely produce a convincing image. Report not only peak distance but also spatial spread, missed activity, wrong confident attributions and uncertainty when competing locations remain plausible. Those quantities answer different questions, as Hauk's resolution analysis illustrates. If the same measured data can support two distant sources under plausible models, the honest output may be **ambiguous**, not a forced bright spot.

I would also separate **agreement between methods** from **agreement with reality**. Two inverse methods may share the same leadfield, MRI registration, covariance and source assumptions. Their maps can agree because they inherit the same error. Methods can also disagree for reasons that have nothing to do with which location was active. An independent endpoint and a test that varies their shared assumptions tell us more than a vote among similar-looking maps.

Finally, the reference itself should be audited. Were scalp electrodes correctly aligned to that person's MRI? Does the implanted contact sample the alleged origin or only a nearby part of a wider network? Does the time window capture the event's beginning or a propagated response? Is a surgical cavity being treated as a point? These details are not footnotes. They determine what “correct location” means.

The experiment I would most like to see combines the strengths of these studies without pretending any one reference is complete. It would use **simultaneous measured scalp and intracranial neural events** across several participants, independently checked individual geometry, a model-uncertainty range fixed in advance, and a source-reporting rule that can explicitly decline to name a unique region. The development participants would set parameters; held-out participants would test them. Direct leadfield comparison, established resolution measures and conventional inverse methods would be matched baselines. The principal outcome would be whether the rule makes **fewer confidently wrong anatomical claims at a stated coverage level**, with intracranial sampling limits reported rather than ignored. A surgical endpoint could provide a separate test of clinical usefulness.

Even that experiment would miss activity outside the implanted contacts. But it would force a source map to survive three tests: an independent neural recording, plausible changes to the head model, and comparison with methods researchers already use. I would trust a location more after those checks than after seeing a convincing image alone.
