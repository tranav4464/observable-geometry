# When Two Brain Locations Look Alike to EEG

*What building Observable Geometry taught me about the limits of a convincing brain map*

**In brief:** I built a tool that compares the scalp signals predicted for distant cortical locations under one EEG model. I could reproduce its rankings and check the calculation independently. The score itself is established mathematics. It tells me where this model predicts ambiguity, not where a person's neurons were active.

Imagine two lights behind a frosted window. From outside, you can see a glow, but a bright light farther away and a dimmer light nearby might leave almost the same pattern on the glass. Adding more detail to your drawing of the glow does not tell you which light was on.

EEG source imaging faces a related problem. Electrodes record voltage differences at the scalp. Researchers then use a head model to estimate which brain locations might have generated those measurements. The resulting maps can be detailed enough to show a small region lighting up. But how distinctly did that region appear to the electrodes in the first place? Could a distant region have produced nearly the same predicted scalp pattern?

I built **Observable Geometry** to check that question before interpreting a source map. Given a leadfield, candidate coordinates, a matching noise covariance and model metadata, it lists distant candidates whose predicted scalp signals are most alike. Electrode geometry and the sensor reference enter through the supplied forward model and covariance; the command does not reconstruct them from raw electrode files. A researcher gets specific alternatives to inspect, along with the model used to produce them.

**Here is what I can test:** for one stated head model and noise model, which distant *single-source candidates* have the most similar predicted scalp patterns? I can answer that without looking at a recorded EEG event. To identify the source of an actual event, I would need different evidence. That distinction shaped the build, its tests and the experiments I decided not to count as validation.

## The trip from brain to scalp, and the trip back

A forward model predicts electrode voltages from a proposed source using assigned head geometry, tissue properties and electrode positions. Its *leadfield* is a table of those predictions: each column lists what the electrodes would measure for one candidate source.

Electrical fields spread through the head before electrodes measure their combined effect. Cortical folds also change current directions. Distant sources can consequently leave similar sensor patterns [1].

The inverse problem estimates sources from scalp measurements. There are more possible source configurations than independent sensor measurements, so an inverse method needs assumptions that favor some solutions. A selected map does not by itself rule out plausible alternatives [1].

The **geometric-informed basis functions** (GBFs) framework was my starting point. It represents source activity using modes, reusable spatial patterns derived from an individual's cortical geometry, and then estimates their contributions to EEG or MEG measurements. The published framework evaluates reconstruction across several kinds of data [2]. I reproduced a bounded implementation path using **20 modes per hemisphere**, compared with **300** in the paper's demonstration. I checked numerical agreement on the same inputs; I did not reproduce the paper's full benchmark [4]. While examining its forward model, I wanted to know where the model predicted lookalike source patterns before an inverse solution was chosen.

The `distinguish` command compares columns of a supplied forward model directly. It does not run GBF source reconstruction. The verified participant example uses fixed-orientation cortical vertices. A researcher could supply another source family, provided its construction and assumptions are documented.

## What does “looks alike” mean?

The calculation begins with a matrix, not an EEG recording. Let **G** have one row for each retained electrode channel and one column for each candidate source. Its column **gᵢ** predicts the channel voltages produced by a unit coefficient at candidate **i** under the supplied head model. For the verified case, **G** has 236 rows and 8,196 columns. Each column represents one fixed current direction at a point on the model's cortical mesh, with every other candidate coefficient set to zero. This tests a *source family*, the patterns the model allows, rather than every possible pattern of brain activity.

The other required matrix is the **236 × 236 noise covariance C**, a description of how background fluctuations tend to move across the electrodes. Its diagonal describes noise at each channel, and its off-diagonal terms describe noise shared between channels. The rows of G and both axes of C must use precisely the same channel names, order and reference. A covariance from a different montage could still have the right dimensions and yield plausible numbers while answering the wrong question. The command checks numerical compatibility. The metadata and preparation record document the intended physical match, which needs its own quality check.

Average referencing subtracts the channel average from every reading. The resulting readings add to zero, so one combination carries no new information. In mathematical terms, the covariance is *rank deficient*. I diagonalize **C = UΛUᵀ**: **U** gives noise directions and **Λ** gives their variances. I retain directions whose variance exceeds **10⁻¹⁰** of the largest. On that supported space, the whitened signature is **zᵢ = Λᵣ⁻¹ᐟ²Uᵣᵀgᵢ**. In plain terms, I rotate the predicted pattern into those noise directions and divide each component by its typical noise size. For this case the supported rank is 235, so the computation compares vectors in a 235-dimensional supported space. The implementation rejects a leadfield with appreciable energy in a discarded direction rather than silently erasing a predicted signal. It also excludes columns whose whitened norm is effectively zero.

Next define **sᵢ = ||zᵢ||₂** and the unit pattern **uᵢ = zᵢ/sᵢ** for each valid candidate. The scalar **sᵢ** is retained in the output, but the principal comparison uses **uᵢ**. Normalization removes differences in predicted strength. A deep source with a weak scalp field and a superficial source with a stronger one can score as similar if their *shapes* match. Without a specified physical source amplitude or signal-to-noise ratio, the score cannot say how difficult it would be to distinguish actual events.

For two candidates, the score is **ρᵢⱼ = |uᵢᵀuⱼ|**. The dot product is a cosine because both vectors have length one: **1** means matching shapes, while **0** means no overlap in this noise-weighted comparison. The absolute value also counts a sign-reversed pattern as a match. If the science requires a known source polarity, meaning a known current direction, that absolute value would be inappropriate and a signed comparison should be used. If orientation is uncertain, one fixed column per location is too restrictive; a free- or loose-orientation source family would require a different comparison. These choices are assumptions visible in the formula, not hidden properties of EEG.

The program also reports **rᵢⱼ = √(1 − ρᵢⱼ²)**. This asks what difference remains after we adjust the competitor's size and sign to fit the target pattern as closely as possible. For example, **ρ = 0.8 gives r = 0.6**, not 0.6 volts.

To derive it, write that adjusted pattern as **a uⱼ**. Expanding **||uᵢ − a uⱼ||₂²** gives **1 − 2a(uᵢᵀuⱼ) + a²**. The minimum occurs at **a = uᵢᵀuⱼ**, leaving **1 − (uᵢᵀuⱼ)²**; taking the square root gives r. A residual of zero means the shapes match perfectly. This is the residual for a *unit whitened target pattern*. It is dimensionless, neither a millimetre localization error nor the residual of a measured EEG event. A physical-amplitude residual would also depend on **sᵢ**, which is why the saved norms matter.

The implementation's [`compute_distinguishability`](https://github.com/tranav4464/observable-geometry/blob/main/src/observable_geometry/track_b/distinguishability.py) function checks input shapes and covariance support, computes **zᵢ** and **uᵢ**, and ranks eligible pairs in blocks. It exposes the retained rank and norms for review. Multiplying C by one positive scalar leaves ρ unchanged; changing its *shape*, channel selection or reference can change it.

The resulting score runs from zero to one. A value near one means the two modeled patterns have nearly the same shape after whitening and allowance for polarity. It does **not** mean there is a 95% chance of localization error when the score is 0.95. That probability would require additional assumptions about source amplitudes, noise, competing sources and an actual inference procedure, followed by empirical calibration. Similar angle-based source-separability ideas appeared in the literature decades ago [1]. I focused on making this particular comparison inspectable: which inputs produced the ranking, which pairs were excluded, and whether another calculation gives the same scores.

The tool excludes candidates within a chosen spatial distance, then ranks the remaining alternatives for each location. That distance is a question the researcher sets, not a universal resolution limit. A source can have a nearby lookalike because neighboring cortical locations are similar; the more surprising case is a high-scoring alternative far away. The CSV preserves the source and candidate indices, their distance and score. A manifest records the model and code hashes, and an access log records which arrays the command opened.

## What happened on a real participant model

The development case is **Localize-MI sub-01**, a dataset collected during simultaneous intracranial stimulation and high-density scalp EEG [5]. The project had a participant-specific forward solution, source coordinates and a noise covariance prepared in its earlier, sealed Track A. The raw forward solution has three orientation components per cortical vertex. The documented preparation selects the 236 accepted scalp channels, converts the forward solution to one direction approximately perpendicular to each cortical patch using MNE's cortical patch statistics, and applies the same common-average reference to the forward and covariance. The resulting fixed-orientation G has one column per vertex. These are model inputs derived from a real participant's anatomy, not scalp measurements of 8,196 known active sources.

The covariance came from the pooled **−200 to −50 ms pre-event interval**, with **10% diagonal shrinkage**, which pulls the estimated noise relationships slightly toward a simpler diagonal matrix, and a stated scaling for an average of **38 trials**. That scaling changes saved absolute whitened norms but not the normalized cosine if it is a global scalar. The pulse event identifies the provenance of this preparation; the `distinguish` command itself reads no event waveform. I did not use the stimulation contact as ground truth for an endogenous neural source.

For every valid query vertex i, the program calculates the Euclidean distance to each other vertex from the supplied coordinates in metres. It excludes itself and candidates closer than **20 mm**, then sorts the survivors by decreasing ρ, breaking exact ties by candidate index, and keeps the first **five**. Twenty millimetres is a reporting choice to focus on non-local alternatives. It is neither the resolution of the electrodes nor distance along the folded cortical sheet. The implementation works in blocks to avoid materializing the entire similarity and distance matrices at once; the block size changes memory use, not the mathematical ranking.

The public command takes a model-only NPZ bundle, a compressed file of numerical arrays, matching metadata and a fresh output directory. It writes `ranked_pairs.csv` for human inspection, `scores.npz` for numerical replay, `summary.json` for descriptive aggregates, `run_manifest.json` for input and code provenance, and `npz_key_access.jsonl` for the array-access record. It refuses to overwrite a previous result. The participant bundle is not redistributed with the public repository because of its dataset license. A new researcher can install and run the public arithmetic example; independently repeating the participant result requires lawful access to the matching model-only inputs.

This one participant model had **236 scalp channels**, **8,196 fixed-orientation candidate cortical vertices** and covariance support rank **235**. At **20 mm** separation with five alternatives per vertex, the command wrote **40,980 directed rows**. These are five rows per source, not 40,980 distinct unordered pairs.

The median best distant similarity was **0.91556**. About **6.04%** of vertices had a best distant similarity of at least **0.95** [4]. The 0.95 cutoff describes the distribution; it has not been validated as a rule for accepting or rejecting a source claim. These numbers describe this model, reference, covariance, source family and distance choice. They are not an estimate of how often EEG localization fails in people.

I wanted every ranked pair to be traceable to its inputs. A clean installation reproduced the participant model's **40,980 ranked rows** and score arrays exactly [4]. A separate calculation reproduced every ranking, with a maximum cosine difference of about **2.1 × 10⁻¹⁵** [4]. The CLI rejects undeclared arrays in its model bundle, including a recorded EEG `data` array, before opening them. These checks support numerical reproducibility and the stated access boundary; they do not validate the anatomy of a recovered neural source.

The verification had several layers. Small algebraic fixtures checked the exact cases of identical, reversed and orthogonal patterns, as well as tie ordering, covariance rescaling and invalid inputs. Those fixtures test arithmetic, not neuroscience. The frozen development calculation and the later higher-precision float64 implementation then agreed exactly on candidate indices, validity masks and eligible-candidate counts; the maximum difference from the saved lower-precision float32 cosine values was **2.98 × 10⁻⁸**, below the prespecified **10⁻⁷** rounding tolerance [4]. A separate CPU calculation replayed the scores at float64 precision. Finally, the installed package in a fresh public clone reproduced the CSV, numerical arrays and summary byte for byte using lawfully held local model inputs. The published repository supplies source code, example inputs and these audit records, while stating which participant inputs a public reader cannot obtain from the package alone.

The checks establish repeatable arithmetic for the declared model. The unit of analysis for the distribution below is **one model vertex**, summarized by its best distant match. The five exported rows per vertex are not independent observations, and neighboring vertices share anatomy and leadfield structure. The validation of the numerical result says nothing about how often a real neural event has the model's assumed single-vertex form.

## The comparison that changed how I describe the tool

Existing source-imaging software can examine resolution through an inverse operator, the calculation that turns scalp measurements into a source estimate. A *point-spread function* asks where that method would place activity from one proposed point source; a *cross-talk function* asks which true sources could contribute to one estimated location. These are established ways to inspect leakage and localization behavior [3]. My diagnostic is calculated before choosing an inverse operator, so it asks a related but different question about the predicted sensor patterns themselves.

I paired the best distant similarity for all **8,196** vertices with point-spread summaries from a minimum-norm method, which favors smaller overall source activity, on the same participant model. Spearman correlation asks whether vertices that rank high under one measure also tend to rank high under another. It was **0.480** with peak-location error and **0.434** with point-spread weighted distance [4]. These moderate associations show that the summaries are related and not identical on this model. They do not show that my output makes a researcher choose correctly more often. The vertices are spatially dependent, and the analysis had no independently verified neural-source endpoint, so I did not turn those correlations into population claims or p-values.

I could have labeled pairs scoring around 0.95 “unresolvable,” but I had no evidence for that cutoff. The list gives a researcher specific alternatives to inspect before comparing two regions. I have not yet shown that seeing the list changes a reporting decision beyond what a direct leadfield-correlation map or standard resolution report would provide. That comparison needs its own experiment.

## Why I stopped some tempting experiments

The Localize-MI data also include electrical stimulation pulses. Those pulses have a known contact, which initially looked attractive as a location reference. But the scalp response to a stimulation artifact is not the same thing as an endogenous neural source event. A channel mismatch in an earlier route also made one comparison invalid. I kept the failure and the corrected rerun in the project record rather than using the pulse result to claim neural-source recovery.

I examined other measured-data routes too. For a rigorous source validation, I would need compatible scalp recordings, reliable participant-specific electrode-to-MRI geometry and an independently defensible endpoint. The WSGZP data offer promising simultaneous scalp and intracranial measurements, but the exact registration provenance and quality checks required for participant-specific source analysis remain unresolved in the material available to this build. Sparse intracranial contacts also sample only part of the brain; they cannot silently become a complete ground-truth source map.

I stopped at a model audit. The measured-data routes I examined did not yet justify claims about endogenous source recovery, population generalization or better researcher decisions. They did show me what a convincing next test would need: checked geometry and a measured neural event with an independent, appropriately limited reference.

## What a researcher can do with it

Consider a study that plans to compare estimated activity in two distant cortical regions. The researcher can supply the same head model, reference, channel set and noise covariance intended for the inverse analysis. Observable Geometry then reports whether those regions contain candidate patterns that appear similar at the sensors. If they do, the report gives concrete pairs and model provenance to inspect while deciding how specific the anatomical interpretation should be.

The researcher still needs the inverse method's resolution analysis, sensitivity checks and empirical evidence appropriate to the claim. My report offers a direct view of the model before those choices. Its usefulness beyond existing diagnostics remains to be tested with a prespecified decision and matched baselines.

The next version should ask whether the ranking survives justified changes in covariance, montage and source orientation, and whether a region-level report helps someone make a better decision than direct leadfield similarity or point-spread analysis alone. An eligible measured-data study would then test whether that decision tracks independently verified neural events. A larger model or a fancier score would not replace those tests.

## What I learned from the build

I started with a broad ambition: find out how much of the brain we can reconstruct from outside the head. Working through GBFs, forward models, inverse resolution and difficult validation datasets made the useful question more precise. A beautiful source map can be computationally valid while the sensors still permit a distant alternative under the chosen model. The first useful step is to make that alternative visible and traceable.

For a declared model, Observable Geometry shows **which distant source patterns the electrodes are predicted to confuse**. I can reproduce that result and explain every step that produced it. I still want to know whether showing those alternatives helps a researcher make a better decision than the tools they already have.
